My name is Muhammad Royhan. I'm a Tech Lead at Moving Bytes Digital, where I've worked since June two thousand nineteen — the only company I've worked for since graduating.

Before diving into the other essays on this site — the philosophy behind inheriting code, or how this website itself was built — I think there should be one piece that answers the most basic question first: who I am, and why I do this work.

## A Brief Background

I didn't get into software engineering out of some childhood calling. The field looked overcrowded, and I had no appetite for competing against people who had wanted it far longer than I had. But Moving Bytes Digital hired me anyway in two thousand nineteen, and the path opened up from there.

Seven years later, I've been promoted three times: from Junior Software Engineer, to Software Engineer, to Senior Software Engineer, and finally to Tech Lead since early two thousand twenty-three. All at the same company.

This isn't a story about job-hopping or chasing the best offer. It's a story about staying in one place long enough to actually understand the consequences of the decisions I make — and seeing firsthand what happens when those decisions are wrong, or right.

## What I Actually Work On

By tech stack, I'm a fullstack engineer working with React and Next Js on the frontend, and Node Js, Express Js, and NestJS on the backend. PostgreSQL and MySQL for databases. I've also built cross-platform mobile apps with Flutter — the first Flutter application my company ever shipped.

But by problem domain, the picture gets more specific: systems where getting the math wrong isn't an option.

I've built and maintained a payroll system for a company with over eight hundred employees. I owned an employee credit system for a rural credit bank, from first commit through handover. Earlier in my career, I also worked on ERP and rental marketplace projects.

The common thread is the same: these are all systems where a wrong number isn't a cosmetic bug. It's a wage that doesn't arrive, a debt recorded incorrectly, a business decision made on data that was never right to begin with.

## Why I Stayed in This Field

I didn't get into this field for love of code. I stayed because people depend on my work being right — and that turned out to be a far stronger reason than passion ever was.

Working on the employee credit system in two thousand twenty-two, I learned that a wrong number on screen is somebody's real debt in the real world. Getting promoted to Team Lead in two thousand twenty-three and being handed responsibility for maintaining older systems, I learned that decisions already made by someone else aren't something I get to control — the only thing I control is whether the next person inherits that system in better shape than I received it.

This way of thinking shapes how I approach every new system: not "how fast can this ship", but "can this be explained six months from now, to someone who has no reason to just trust me".

## My Role as Tech Lead

Since two thousand twenty-three, my responsibility hasn't just been writing code. I manage two projects at once with two software engineers, from planning through production deployment. I've designed reusable backend architecture adopted across multiple client projects — something that's measurably cut setup time for new work.

I'm also the person clients come to for technical consultation, and I'm the one who translates client requirements into clear tasks for the engineering team. Beyond the payroll work, between March and June two thousand twenty-five, the company trusted me with a cross-country initiative — a kind of test of whether I could hold my own next to engineers from other countries.

The part I actually enjoy about this role isn't having more authority — it's having more context. When you're leading, you see how a small technical decision on one project ripples into another, how one architectural choice today becomes either a burden or a foundation six months out.

## How I Think About the Work

There are two principles I hold onto, and both will keep showing up in other essays on this site.

First, systems thinking — understanding a system by how its parts affect each other over time, not by inspecting any one part in isolation. The payroll system I built isn't just a salary calculator. It's a network of tax rules, company policy, employee data that changes every month, and audit requirements that have to be answerable at any point.

Second, a bias toward what's controllable — a principle from Stoic philosophy that I use as a working tool, not a motivational quote. I can't control decisions a previous team already made, a deadline a client set, or whether a third-party API stays up. What I control is how I design the system to hold up regardless.

## Why I'm Writing This

This site isn't a dressed-up resume. It's where I document the thinking I actually use to make decisions — whether that's an architectural call on the payroll system, a design decision while building this very website, or the judgment calls I made inheriting someone else's legacy system.

The essays that follow will go deeper into each of these: how I approach code I inherit, how I collaborate with tools to build something like this site, and eventually, the concrete technical decisions behind the systems I've mentioned above.

But it all starts from the same place: seven years in one company, building systems that can't afford to be wrong, and learning that the one thing I actually control is whether the next person inherits my work in clearer shape than I found it.
