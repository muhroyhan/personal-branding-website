## The constraint

My first real system was one someone else had already started. A two-sided
rental marketplace — renters on one side, item owners on the other, the
platform mediating between them — handed over mid-run, with its decisions
already made and its production users already on it.

Nothing about that was negotiable. I could not rewrite it, could not re-argue
its architecture, and could not pause it to understand it first. The only
variable I actually controlled was whether it was more explainable when I
handed it on than when I received it.

That framing is the reason this project is on the site at all. Everything I
have designed since is a reaction to it.

## What a marketplace actually is

The naive model of an order is a sequence of clicks: pay, confirm, ship,
receive, return. That model survives about a week in production.

The real lifecycle has roughly a dozen states, and **most transitions between
them are not clicks**. They are timeouts, third-party webhooks, and scheduled
jobs firing at 3am. A renter who never pays, a vendor who never confirms, an
item that is never returned — none of those produce an event. The absence *is*
the event, and the system has to notice it on its own.

Once you see that, the architecture stops being about screens.

### Cancellation is a clock, not a button

An order can die from silence at four different points: awaiting payment,
pending ID verification, awaiting vendor confirmation, and waiting for
delivery. Each has its own deadline, and the deadlines are not uniform because
the promises made to the renter are not uniform:

- **Instant booking** — the vendor has one hour to confirm, one hour from the
  booking start to deliver.
- **Standard booking** — 24 hours for both, but *counted only during store
  opening hours*. A vendor who is closed is not a vendor who is ignoring you.
- **Delayed booking** — 24 hours, regardless of opening hours.

That opening-hours clause is the part I would point at in an interview. It is
trivial to implement and impossible to guess from a requirements document; it
exists because a rule that punishes vendors for being closed on Sunday is
technically correct and commercially wrong.

### Two couriers, two vocabularies, one order status

Deliveries ran through two third-party providers, and they do not agree on what
a delivery is. One reports `Finding Driver → Out For Pickup → Out For Delivery
→ Completed`. The other reports `locating_driver → driver_accept_booking →
delivery_in_progress → delivery_completed`. Different granularity, different
names, different failure modes.

Both had to land on the same internal order status, because a renter should
never have to learn which courier the vendor picked. So courier state was
translated at the boundary rather than leaked into the domain — the order model
knows *waiting for delivery, out for delivery, renting*, and nothing about
whose driver is carrying the item.

The failure paths mattered more than the happy path, and they were not
symmetrical either:

- If either courier **cancels** or **no driver is found**, the "find driver"
  action is re-enabled so the vendor can try again. A dead order held hostage
  by a courier is a support ticket.
- If one provider **times out** locating a driver, its own support team picks
  the booking up manually. In that case the button must *stay disabled* —
  re-enabling it would let the vendor book a second driver for an order that is
  already being rescued.

Same-shaped events, opposite correct responses. That distinction only exists
because someone read both integration contracts properly, and it is the kind of
detail that never appears in a ticket.

### The promo refund problem

The hardest problem in the system was not delivery. It was this: an order
contains three items, a promo code was applied because the combined total
cleared a minimum, and now the renter cancels one item.

What do you refund?

Refund the full item price, and the remaining order no longer meets the
minimum the discount was granted for — the renter has kept a discount they no
longer qualify for. Refund item minus discount, and someone who cancelled a
cheap item is silently penalised for a discount that was earned by the
expensive ones.

There is no answer that is correct from every angle, which is exactly why it
had sat as a known bug. What shipped was an explicit, defensible rule rather
than an elegant one:

- If the promo is **not** tied to a specific store or category, the discount is
  charged in full against the first cancelled item; subsequent cancellations
  refund at face value.
- If the promo **is** store- or category-specific, the discount is charged only
  against the item that actually earned it.
- If that leaves a refund in the negative, the platform balance is allowed to
  go negative and carries into the next order — and while it is negative, the
  renter cannot withdraw.

I do not think it is the best possible rule. I do think it is a rule you can
state in one sentence to a support agent, which the previous behaviour was
not.

### Failure gets a recovery path, not a log line

Two patterns I still reuse came from here.

Overdue rentals had an insurance policy that a scheduled job extended one day
at a time until the item came back. That job talks to a third party, so it will
eventually fail. Rather than let it fail into a log nobody reads, failed
extensions surface **on the admin dashboard with a re-run button** — the people
who notice the problem are given the means to fix it, without an engineer in
the loop.

The same instinct applies to the cancellation counter: a vendor gets three
free cancellations a year, and past that, cancelling forces re-verification and
closes their store until an admin reopens it. The counter resets on 1 January,
by cron. A policy nobody has to remember to enforce is the only kind that
actually gets enforced.

## What it taught

I inherited this system, and later I was promoted specifically to look after
systems like it — code I had not written, decisions I had not made, timelines
I had not set.

The lesson took years to state properly, and it is the spine of this whole
site: **you rarely control what you inherit. You only control whether it is
more explainable when you hand it on.** The clearest thing I left behind was
not a refactor. It was a written system manual — order lifecycle, cron
inventory, courier status mappings, notification matrix, error codes — so the
next person could answer their own questions.

That instinct hardened into a design constraint at the
[bank](/work/core-banking-bpr) a year later, and into an architectural
requirement in the [payroll engine](/work/payroll-system) after that.

## Result

- Maintained and extended the marketplace from 2019 through 2021, having
  inherited it mid-build.
- Built the Flutter mobile app alongside the web platform, in a team.
- Authored the system manual the platform was maintained from — the order
  state machine, twelve scheduled jobs, three webhook integrations, and the
  full notification matrix across email, WhatsApp and push.
- Shipped the promo cancellation-refund logic that closed a standing bug in
  partial-order refunds.

## What I would do differently

I treated the scheduled jobs as a list of independent tasks, because that is
how I found them. They were not — several of them were transitions in a single
order state machine, split across separate lambdas and separate schedules,
which is why reasoning about "how can an order end up here" meant reading five
files.

Today I would name the state machine explicitly in one place and let the jobs
be thin triggers into it. Not for elegance: so that the answer to *what can
happen to an order* lives somewhere a person can read, rather than being
distributed across a crontab.
