## The constraint

In 2022 I took a credit system for a *Bank Perkreditan Rakyat* — an Indonesian
rural credit bank — from first commit through to handover. It was the first
project I owned end to end, and the least forgiving domain I had worked in.

The product is employee lending. A company partners with the bank; its staff
apply for credit against their salary; the company's HR verifies that the
applicant is who they say they are and earns what they claim; the bank
underwrites and approves. Three parties, three portals, one system.

Banking software has no cosmetic bugs. A misplaced figure is somebody's debt,
and the people it belongs to find out before you do. There is no staging
environment for a customer's loan.

## What the system actually produces

Most systems output a screen. This one outputs a **contract**.

At the end of a successful application the system generates the *PKS* — the
credit agreement between the bank, the employer and the borrower — carrying the
principal, the tenor, the monthly instalment, the interest, and the additional
fees. That document is the thing people sign. Everything upstream of it exists
to make sure it is right.

Two consequences fell out of that, and they shaped the build more than any
framework choice.

**The commercial terms are per-tenant data, not code.** Each partner company is
onboarded with its own interest table by tenor and its own fee schedule.
Instalments are derived from those, and the generated agreement reflects the
terms configured for that specific employer. Hard-coding a rate would have
meant a deploy every time the bank negotiated a new partner — and, worse, a
class of bug where the number on screen and the number in the contract could
disagree.

**Rejection has to be terminal and quiet.** An application rejected by the
employer never reaches the bank's queue at all. That is a one-line rule, and it
is the difference between a bank underwriter reviewing live applications and
one wading through cases the employer already killed.

## The approval chain

The flow is deliberately linear and deliberately interruptible:

```
borrower applies
  → employer's HR approves or rejects   (reject → terminal)
  → bank reviews, may adjust terms, approves or rejects
  → borrower notified, confirms or walks away
  → borrower supplies bank account
  → agreement (PKS) generated
```

Every stage can end the process, and each stage belongs to a different party
with different authority. The bank can amend the amount and the insurance cost;
the employer cannot. The borrower can decline after approval, and that is a
legitimate outcome rather than an error state — approving credit is not the
same as taking it.

Notification at the approval step goes out over WhatsApp, because the borrower
is a factory or office employee who is not sitting in the portal waiting. The
system does not assume its users are watching it.

## What actually shaped the design

The technical work was ordinary: multi-tenant portals, role-scoped access,
document capture for KYC, approvals, exports, generated agreements. What was
not ordinary was the ending. This system was going to be handed to another
team, and I was not going to be there to explain it.

That reframed almost every decision. The question stopped being "is this
correct?" and became **"is this correct in a way the next person can verify
without me?"** Those are not the same requirement, and the second one is
strictly harder:

- **Explicit over clever.** Anywhere a concise expression and an obvious one
  disagreed, I took the obvious one. Cleverness is a loan against someone
  else's time.
- **Domain language in the code.** The names in the schema and the services
  match the names the bank's staff use — *debitor*, *angsuran*, *PKS*. A
  maintainer who does not know the codebase but does know the domain can still
  navigate it.
- **Boundaries where the handover would cut.** Modules were drawn along lines
  a new team could take ownership of one at a time — bank admin, employer
  admin, borrower — rather than along the lines that were most convenient while
  I was the only one writing.

Access control got the same treatment. Bank administrators are granted
read/write per feature rather than by role bundle, and the system refuses to
let the last remaining administrator be deleted. A permission model that can
lock every human out of the bank's own console is not a security feature.

## What it taught

The handover taught me more than the build did.

Code you hand to someone else has to explain itself without you in the room.
Once you take that seriously, it stops being a documentation problem and
becomes an architecture problem — the structure either makes the reasoning
visible or it does not, and no README rescues one that does not.

Every system I have designed since has been built against that constraint,
including the [payroll engine](/work/payroll-system) two years later. The
requirement that every figure be re-derivable from its inputs is the same idea,
stated in a domain where the stakes made it non-negotiable.

## Result

- Delivered and handed over to a separate maintenance team in 2022.
- First project I owned from first commit to handover, at Senior Software
  Engineer level.
- Multi-tenant by design: each partner employer runs on its own portal path,
  its own interest and fee configuration, and its own borrower pool.
- Established the design constraint — *explainable after handover* — that the
  rest of my work has been built on.

## What I would do differently

Loan settlement was left as a manual status change by a bank administrator. At
the time that was the honest choice — repayment happens through payroll
deduction outside the system, so the system genuinely does not know. But
"a human will remember to click this" is a design decision, not the absence of
one, and I documented it as a limitation rather than solving it.

The version I would build now still would not guess at settlement. It would
make the gap visible — flag loans whose scheduled term has elapsed while the
status has not moved — so that the manual step is prompted by the system rather
than remembered by a person.
